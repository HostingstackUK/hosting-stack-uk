How Much Should Small Businesses Pay for WordPress Hosting?

Published: 25 July 2026Last fact-checked: 25 July 2026Reading time: Approximately 11 minutes

A small business can pay anywhere from a few pounds to well over £100 per month for WordPress hosting. The appropriate amount depends less on the size of the business than on the importance, complexity and workload of its website.

A basic informational website may perform adequately on a reputable entry-level plan. A busy ecommerce store, membership platform or lead-generation website may require more processing capacity, stronger recovery tools and specialist support.

These are editorial budgeting ranges rather than fixed market prices. Hosting plans differ significantly, and advertised introductory prices often do not reflect renewal charges, VAT, optional services or the cost of longer contracts.

Opening budget summary

  • £5–£15 per month for a small informational website with modest traffic
  • £15–£40 per month for an established business website requiring stronger features and support
  • £40–£100+ per month for an ecommerce, membership or business-critical WordPress website

Key takeaway

A small business should pay enough for hosting that supports the website’s commercial importance, but it should not purchase expensive infrastructure that the website does not yet need.

The Simple Answer

A typical small business brochure website should usually budget approximately £10–£30 per month for dependable WordPress hosting.

That range can provide more room to evaluate:

  • Automatic backups
  • SSL certificates
  • WordPress updates
  • Security features
  • Customer support
  • Storage and traffic allowances
  • Caching
  • Migration assistance
  • Reliable renewal terms

The lowest-priced plan may still be suitable for a new or non-critical website. However, a business should be cautious about choosing hosting solely because it costs £1 or £2 per month during an introductory period.

For example, SiteGround’s UK WordPress hosting page advertised an introductory price of £1.99 per month for its entry plan when this article was checked, but listed a renewal price of £13.99 per month, prepaid annually and excluding VAT.[1] Hostinger similarly displayed discounted long-term pricing alongside substantially higher renewal rates.[2]

The correct comparison is therefore the total ongoing cost, not just the price shown most prominently in an advertisement.

Why WordPress Hosting Prices Vary So Much

WordPress itself is open-source software, but it requires a suitable web server to operate. WordPress documentation distinguishes between general-purpose hosting, which can support different applications, and WordPress-specific hosting that may include additional tools and services.[3]

Hosting prices vary because plans may provide different levels of:

  • Processing capacity
  • Memory
  • Storage
  • Network transfer
  • Account isolation
  • Backup retention
  • Security
  • Technical support
  • WordPress management
  • Staging facilities
  • Development tools
  • Service monitoring

Two plans described as “WordPress hosting” may therefore offer very different services.

One may be a low-cost shared account with WordPress pre-installed. Another may be a managed platform with automated backups, specialist support, integrated caching, staging and monitoring.

The product name alone does not reveal whether a plan represents good value.

A Practical WordPress Hosting Budget for Small Businesses

£5–£15 per month: basic business hosting

This range may be sufficient for:

  • A new local-business website
  • A simple brochure site
  • A small portfolio
  • A restaurant or tradesperson website
  • A website receiving relatively modest traffic
  • A business that does not process transactions online

At this level, look for:

  • Free SSL
  • Adequate storage
  • Automatic backups
  • Current PHP support
  • WordPress installation tools
  • Clear resource limits
  • Responsive customer support
  • Transparent renewal pricing

A plan in this range does not need every advanced feature. However, the business should still be able to recover its website if an update, error or security incident causes a problem.

WordPress describes backups as essential and advises retaining copies of both website files and the database.[4]

Suitable when

This budget may be reasonable when the website:

  • Contains mostly static pages
  • Has a limited number of plugins
  • Does not handle customer accounts
  • Is updated occasionally
  • Is not the business’s primary sales channel
  • Can tolerate a short period of disruption

Potential limitations

Lower-cost plans may provide:

  • Weekly rather than daily backups
  • Shorter backup retention
  • Limited storage or processing capacity
  • No staging environment
  • General rather than WordPress-specialist support
  • Higher renewal prices
  • Longer minimum commitments

Check the actual plan conditions rather than assuming that a low introductory price will continue indefinitely.

£15–£40 per month: established small-business hosting

This range is likely to suit many established small businesses.

It may be appropriate for:

  • Lead-generation websites
  • Professional-services firms
  • Growing local businesses
  • Content-led business websites
  • Websites updated regularly
  • Sites running several important plugins
  • Businesses that depend on online enquiries

At this level, a business should expect more than basic storage and an SSL certificate.

Useful features may include:

  • Daily backups
  • Simple backup restoration
  • Improved caching
  • Malware or vulnerability monitoring
  • Staging
  • Migration assistance
  • More generous resources
  • Better support
  • Development access such as SFTP or SSH
  • Content delivery network integration

For example, SiteGround’s higher WordPress plans list features such as staging, Git access, caching, backups and developer tools, although features and prices vary by package.[5]

This does not mean that every business needs that provider or those particular tools. It demonstrates why two hosting plans at different prices may not be directly comparable.

Suitable when

This budget may be justified when:

  • Enquiries generated through the website have real commercial value
  • The website changes frequently
  • Several employees or contractors manage it
  • A broken update would disrupt marketing or sales
  • The owner wants easier backups and restoration
  • The business would benefit from staging
  • Reliable support is worth paying for

£40–£100+ per month: business-critical or specialist hosting

Higher-cost managed WordPress hosting may be appropriate for:

  • WooCommerce stores
  • Membership websites
  • Online-course platforms
  • High-traffic publications
  • Booking websites
  • Businesses running paid advertising to landing pages
  • Websites processing frequent customer activity
  • Sites maintained by agencies or development teams

These websites can generate more uncached requests, database activity and background processing than a simple informational site.

The price may include:

  • WordPress-specific infrastructure
  • Automated daily backups
  • Staging environments
  • Integrated caching
  • Performance monitoring
  • Specialist migrations
  • Security controls
  • WordPress-focused support
  • More advanced management tools

Kinsta’s managed WordPress plans were advertised from $30 per month when this article was checked, with pricing shown in US dollars and excluding tax.[6][7] Its documentation describes plan limits based on factors such as WordPress installations, visits, storage and bandwidth.[8]

A premium plan is not automatically necessary for every small business. It becomes easier to justify when the website’s reliability, speed and recovery options have a direct effect on revenue or operations.

The Website’s Importance Matters More Than the Company’s Size

A small company can operate a highly demanding website, while a larger company may only need a simple online brochure.

Consider two examples.

Business A: local electrician

The website contains:

  • Five service pages
  • A contact form
  • A gallery
  • A small number of monthly visitors
  • No customer accounts or payments

A reputable shared or entry-level managed plan may be sufficient.

Business B: specialist online retailer

The website contains:

  • Hundreds of products
  • Customer accounts
  • Online payments
  • Stock-management integrations
  • Regular promotions
  • Paid advertising campaigns
  • Frequent database activity

Although both companies may be classed as small businesses, Business B has considerably greater hosting requirements.

The hosting budget should reflect:

  • The website’s technical workload
  • Its importance to revenue
  • The cost of failure
  • The amount of data changing between backups
  • The level of support the business needs

Calculate the Cost of Website Failure

A useful way to set a hosting budget is to consider what poor hosting could cost the business.

Ask:

  • How many enquiries does the website generate?
  • What is an average customer worth?
  • Does the business sell directly online?
  • What would one day of disruption cost?
  • Could data entered since the last backup be lost?
  • Would staff need to pay a developer for emergency help?
  • Could a slow website reduce advertising results?
  • Would a security incident damage customer confidence?

Suppose a business earns £2,000 per month through website-generated enquiries. Saving £10 per month on hosting may be insignificant if unreliable infrastructure causes missed leads or prolonged downtime.

Conversely, a website that generates no direct income and receives little traffic may not benefit meaningfully from a £100-per-month plan.

The correct budget is based on proportionate risk, not fear-based purchasing.

Do Not Judge Hosting by Traffic Alone

Monthly visitor figures can be useful, but they do not fully describe a website’s workload.

A cached page viewed 20,000 times may require fewer resources than a smaller site performing:

  • Product searches
  • Checkout calculations
  • Membership authentication
  • Personalised dashboards
  • Database filtering
  • Scheduled imports
  • External API requests
  • Frequent background tasks

Consider:

  • Simultaneous visitors
  • Cached versus uncached activity
  • Number and quality of plugins
  • Database size
  • Ecommerce transactions
  • Background processing
  • External integrations
  • Administrative activity

A small business should not automatically upgrade because it reaches an arbitrary visitor number. It should investigate how the website actually uses resources and whether the current plan is reaching its limits.

Eight Costs to Consider Beyond the Advertised Price

1. Renewal pricing

Introductory discounts can reduce the first payment considerably, but the renewal charge may be several times higher.

SiteGround and Hostinger both display promotional prices alongside higher regular or renewal costs on their UK pricing pages.[1][2]

Calculate the cost over at least two or three years.

PeriodIllustrative cost
First year at £3 per month£36
Second year at £12 per month£144
Third year at £12 per month£144
Three-year total£324
Effective monthly cost£9

The £3 headline price does not represent the effective long-term cost.

2. VAT

Some hosting pages display prices excluding VAT, while others may include it or calculate tax during checkout.

SiteGround’s displayed UK prices, for example, state that they exclude VAT.[1]

Verify the final checkout cost before comparing providers.

3. Contract length

A low monthly rate may require payment for:

  • 12 months
  • 24 months
  • 36 months
  • 48 months

Long commitments reduce flexibility. Consider whether the business is comfortable paying the full amount upfront and whether refunds are available.

4. Domain registration

Some providers include a domain for the first year but charge for renewal later.

The domain should remain registered in the business owner’s name and under an account the business controls.

5. Email hosting

Email may be included with shared hosting but excluded from specialist managed WordPress platforms.

The business may need a separate service such as Microsoft 365, Google Workspace or another mail provider. Include this in the total technology budget.

6. Backups

Check whether backups are:

  • Included
  • Automatic
  • Daily or weekly
  • Retained long enough
  • Downloadable
  • Restorable without a fee
  • Stored separately from the live website

WordPress explains that a complete recovery process may require both database and file backups.[4]

7. Security and malware assistance

A hosting provider may detect malware without removing it. Another may offer remediation within the plan, while a lower-cost service may refer the customer to a paid security provider.

Ask what happens after a website is compromised, not merely whether malware scanning exists.

8. Developer or maintenance costs

Hosting does not usually include unlimited WordPress development.

A business may still need help with:

  • Plugin conflicts
  • Theme problems
  • Content updates
  • Broken layouts
  • Custom code
  • Ecommerce configuration
  • Third-party integrations

A more expensive managed host may reduce some technical work, but it does not replace a web developer or maintenance service.

Which Hosting Features Are Worth Paying For?

Automatic backups

Backups are one of the most important features for a business website.

Look for:

  • Daily automatic backups
  • Both files and database included
  • Simple restoration
  • Adequate retention
  • Off-site or separately stored copies
  • Downloadable backups

The more frequently a website’s data changes, the more frequently it may need to be backed up.

A brochure website could potentially tolerate losing one day of edits. An online store may not be able to tolerate losing a day of orders.

Reliable restoration

Do not only ask whether backups exist. Ask how they are restored.

A useful backup feature should make it clear:

  • Who can initiate a restoration
  • How long the process normally takes
  • Whether restoration costs extra
  • Whether a pre-restoration backup is created
  • Whether individual files can be recovered
  • Whether staging restoration is possible

Security controls

Potentially valuable protections include:

  • Free SSL
  • Web application firewall
  • DDoS mitigation
  • Account isolation
  • Malware monitoring
  • Brute-force protection
  • Multi-factor authentication
  • Secure SFTP access
  • Current PHP versions

Kinsta, for example, documents infrastructure protection involving Cloudflare, a managed firewall and DDoS controls, while also explaining that the customer retains responsibility for areas such as account security and website software.[9][10]

The important principle is that security remains a shared responsibility.

Staging

A staging environment allows updates and changes to be tested away from the public website.

It can be especially useful for:

  • WooCommerce updates
  • Theme changes
  • PHP upgrades
  • Plugin replacements
  • Custom development
  • Major page redesigns

Businesses that regularly change their website may reasonably pay more for a simple and dependable staging workflow.

WordPress-specific support

Specialist support can be valuable when it helps diagnose:

  • PHP errors
  • Caching problems
  • Database issues
  • Migration difficulties
  • Server configuration
  • Resource usage
  • WordPress-specific failures

However, check the provider’s support boundaries. Hosting support may identify a plugin conflict without correcting the plugin itself.

Performance tools

Integrated caching, CDN services and performance monitoring can reduce the number of separate systems the business must configure.

They are most valuable when:

  • Properly included in the price
  • Suitable for the website
  • Easy to manage
  • Supported by the provider
  • Not used to justify an unnecessarily expensive package

Features Small Businesses May Be Overpaying For

A small business might be paying too much when it purchases:

  • Capacity far beyond its current workload
  • Support services it never uses
  • A large number of website installations it does not need
  • Enterprise features intended for complex organisations
  • Excessive storage for a lightweight website
  • Advanced development tools without a development workflow
  • Premium traffic allowances for a low-traffic site

Avoid upgrading only because a plan is labelled:

  • Business
  • Professional
  • Premium
  • Enterprise-ready
  • High performance
  • Unlimited

Focus on measurable requirements.

“Unlimited” plans usually remain subject to acceptable-use policies, technical limits or resource controls. Read the provider’s terms and technical documentation.

Signs a Hosting Plan Is Too Cheap for the Business

A low-cost plan may no longer be appropriate when:

  • The website repeatedly reaches resource limits
  • Pages become slow during normal business activity
  • Backups are unreliable or difficult to restore
  • The plan does not support current PHP or WordPress requirements
  • Important updates cannot be tested safely
  • Customer support cannot address hosting-related WordPress problems
  • The business needs to purchase several essential add-ons
  • Website problems regularly require emergency developer assistance
  • Security and account-isolation arrangements are unclear
  • The renewal price is no longer competitive

The solution is not necessarily premium managed hosting. It could be a better shared plan, a different provider or a maintenance agreement with a developer.

Signs a Business May Be Paying Too Much

The hosting plan may be excessive when:

  • Resource usage remains consistently low
  • The website is a simple brochure
  • No premium management tools are used
  • Included staging and developer tools are unnecessary
  • The business pays for many unused installations
  • A less expensive plan would provide the same essential features
  • The plan was chosen based on fear rather than measured requirements

Before downgrading, verify that the lower plan still provides:

  • Adequate backups
  • Suitable performance
  • Current software support
  • Appropriate security
  • Enough resources
  • An acceptable support level

Saving money is only valuable when it does not create disproportionate operational risk.

Should a Small Business Choose Managed WordPress Hosting?

Managed WordPress hosting may be worth the additional cost when the business wants to reduce infrastructure administration.

It can be especially suitable when:

  • The website directly generates revenue
  • Downtime has a meaningful cost
  • The business lacks internal technical expertise
  • Staging and restoration are important
  • The website changes frequently
  • Specialist WordPress support is valuable
  • The site is growing
  • An agency or developer actively manages it

Managed hosting may not be necessary when:

  • The website is small and rarely changes
  • Traffic is modest
  • The owner is comfortable maintaining WordPress
  • A reputable shared plan already performs well
  • The business does not need staging or advanced tools
  • The higher cost would displace more important spending

For a fuller explanation, read Managed WordPress Hosting vs Shared Hosting: A Detailed Comparison.

Questions to Ask a Hosting Provider

Before purchasing, ask:

Pricing

  • What is the renewal price?
  • Is VAT included?
  • How long is the minimum term?
  • Is the full amount paid upfront?
  • Are refunds available?
  • Are there overage charges?

Resources

  • How much storage is included?
  • Are CPU or memory limits disclosed?
  • Is bandwidth limited?
  • How many websites are allowed?
  • What happens when limits are exceeded?

Backups

  • How often are backups created?
  • How long are they retained?
  • Are files and databases both included?
  • Can the business restore them directly?
  • Does restoration cost extra?
  • Can backups be downloaded?

Security

  • Is SSL included?
  • Is malware scanning included?
  • Is malware removal included?
  • Is a firewall provided?
  • Are accounts isolated?
  • Is multi-factor authentication available?

Support

  • Which support channels are available?
  • Is support available outside business hours?
  • Does support cover WordPress?
  • What is outside its scope?
  • Is migration assistance included?

Website management

  • Is staging included?
  • Are updates managed?
  • Is caching configured?
  • Is a CDN included?
  • Are any plugins prohibited?
  • Can the website be exported easily?

Document the answers before committing to a long contract.

A Sensible Buying Process

Step 1: Define the website’s role

Determine whether the website is primarily:

  • An online brochure
  • A source of enquiries
  • An ecommerce store
  • A membership service
  • A publication
  • A booking platform

Step 2: Estimate the cost of disruption

Consider the likely financial and operational effect of:

  • Downtime
  • Data loss
  • Slow performance
  • Security incidents
  • Failed updates

Step 3: Identify essential features

Separate features into:

  • Essential
  • Useful
  • Unnecessary

Step 4: Compare renewal costs

Calculate at least a two- or three-year total, including:

  • Hosting
  • VAT
  • Domain renewal
  • Email
  • Backups
  • Security services
  • Migration
  • Maintenance

Step 5: Start with enough capacity, not maximum capacity

Choose a plan that supports the current website with reasonable headroom.

Confirm that upgrading later is straightforward.

Step 6: Monitor the website

Review:

  • Uptime
  • Speed
  • Resource usage
  • Errors
  • Backup success
  • Traffic growth
  • Support quality

Upgrade when the evidence supports it.

Conclusion

Final Recommendation

For most small UK businesses running a standard WordPress website, £10–£30 per month is a sensible hosting budget.

A newer or less important website may be adequately served by a reputable plan costing £5–£15 per month. A business-critical, ecommerce or membership website may justify £40–£100 or more per month where stronger resources, backups, staging and specialist support reduce meaningful operational risks.

Do not select hosting using price alone.

Evaluate:

  • The normal renewal cost
  • What is included
  • Resource limitations
  • Backup and restoration arrangements
  • Security responsibilities
  • Support scope
  • Website workload
  • The commercial cost of failure

The best-value plan is not necessarily the cheapest or most expensive. It is the one that meets the business’s present requirements, provides an acceptable recovery process and allows the website to grow without paying prematurely for unnecessary capacity.

Research and Editorial Note

This article was prepared using official WordPress documentation and publicly available hosting-provider pricing and service information.

The suggested budget ranges are editorial guidance developed to help small businesses evaluate hosting expenditure. They are not industry-standard prices or guarantees that a particular level of spending will provide specific performance.
