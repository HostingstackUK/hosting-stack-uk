When Should You Upgrade From Shared Hosting?

Published: 25 July 2026Last fact-checked: 25 July 2026Reading time: Approximately 11 minutes

Shared hosting is often an excellent starting point for WordPress websites. It is affordable, widely available and usually provides enough resources for personal websites, portfolios, blogs and many small business sites.

However, every hosting plan has practical limits. As a website attracts more visitors, becomes more complex or grows in commercial importance, there may come a point where remaining on a shared hosting plan begins to affect performance, reliability or workflow.

Upgrading too early can result in paying for resources you do not need. Waiting too long can increase the risk of slow loading times, downtime and lost business opportunities.

This guide explains how to recognise when it may be time to move beyond shared hosting and what factors should influence that decision.

Key takeaway

The best time to upgrade is when your website's requirements consistently exceed the capabilities of your current hosting—not simply because traffic has increased or a provider recommends a more expensive plan.

What Is Shared Hosting?

Shared hosting places multiple customer websites on the same physical server.

Each account receives a share of the available resources, which may include:

  • CPU processing
  • Memory (RAM)
  • Storage
  • Network bandwidth
  • Disk input/output
  • PHP workers
  • Database resources

The exact allocation depends on the hosting provider. Many reputable shared hosting companies use technologies such as account isolation and resource controls to improve stability between customers.

WordPress identifies shared hosting as a common and suitable option for many websites, particularly those with modest requirements.[1]

For many new websites, shared hosting offers excellent value.

Why Shared Hosting Has Limits

A shared server supports multiple customers simultaneously. Although each account is normally subject to resource controls, every website still operates within the provider's overall infrastructure.

As a website grows, it may require more CPU time, memory, simultaneous PHP processes, faster database access, more frequent backups, additional storage, better caching and more specialised support.

Eventually, these requirements may exceed what an entry-level shared hosting plan is designed to provide. That does not necessarily mean shared hosting is “bad”—only that the website has outgrown the original environment.

Traffic Alone Should Not Determine an Upgrade

One of the biggest misconceptions is that a website must upgrade after reaching a particular visitor count. There is no universal threshold.

Website AWebsite B
40,000 monthly page views5,000 monthly visitors
Mostly static pagesWooCommerce
Effective cachingMembership accounts
Few pluginsProduct filtering and booking
Simple themeMultiple third-party integrations

Website B may require more server resources despite attracting fewer visitors.

The correct question is not “How many visitors do I have?” Instead ask: “Can my current hosting consistently support the workload created by those visitors?”

Signs You May Need to Upgrade

1. Website performance becomes inconsistent

Potential symptoms include slow page generation, a delayed administration dashboard, long checkout times, slow database queries and increased Time to First Byte (TTFB). Before blaming hosting, investigate plugin performance, theme efficiency, image optimisation, caching and external scripts. Core Web Vitals can help measure real-world page experience.[4]

2. Resource limits are reached regularly

Common limits include CPU, memory, concurrent processes, PHP workers, I/O usage and entry processes. One isolated spike is less significant than a consistent pattern.

3. Your website has become business-critical

When a website begins generating meaningful revenue, enquiries or bookings, reliability often becomes more valuable than the lowest possible hosting cost.

4. You need staging environments

A staging environment allows plugin, theme, WooCommerce and custom-code updates to be tested safely. WordPress recognises staging as a distinct environment type.[2]

5. Backup requirements increase

Growing websites may need more frequent backups, longer retention, easier restoration, staging restoration and downloadable copies. Complete WordPress backups should include files and the database.[3]

6. Your website regularly exceeds available resources

CPU throttling, memory exhaustion, process limits and database bottlenecks can produce 503 errors, timeouts, failed requests and slow administration.

7. Customer support no longer meets your needs

More complex sites may need help with WordPress configuration, performance, migrations, caching, staging, PHP compatibility and deployment.

8. Multiple websites become difficult to manage

Freelancers and agencies may need central management, staging, client permissions, migrations and collaboration tools.

9. Security requirements increase

Growing businesses may need stronger account isolation, malware monitoring, firewall services, easier restoration, activity logging and better access controls. Security remains a shared responsibility.

10. Growth becomes difficult

Approaching storage limits, no staging, limited backup retention, no developer tools or restricted PHP options can constrain future growth.

Situations Where Shared Hosting Is Still Suitable

Shared hosting remains an excellent option for personal blogs, portfolios, local businesses, charity websites, informational websites, small brochure sites, new businesses and low-traffic websites.

If performance is good and resources remain comfortably below available limits, there may be little reason to upgrade.

The cheapest hosting is not always best—but neither is the most expensive.

Should You Upgrade to VPS Hosting?

A Virtual Private Server commonly offers greater resource allocation, more configuration options, increased control and predictable performance.

However, unless it is fully managed, owners may become responsible for operating-system updates, server configuration, security patches, monitoring and troubleshooting. A VPS is not automatically the next step for every website.

Should You Choose Managed WordPress Hosting Instead?

Managed hosting commonly includes automatic backups, staging, WordPress-specific support, server-level caching, an integrated CDN and performance monitoring.[5]

These features may simplify website management rather than simply increasing available resources.

Common Upgrade Triggers

Ecommerce growth
More products, customers, orders and payment requests usually increase database activity.
Membership websites
Logged-in users generate more dynamic content than cached public pages.
Large media libraries
Thousands of uploaded files may increase storage requirements and backup times.
Increased development
Regular testing, deployments and custom code often benefit from staging and developer tools.[6]
Marketing campaigns
Businesses running advertising campaigns may require hosting that handles sudden traffic increases more consistently.

Signs You Should Optimise Before Upgrading

Not every performance problem requires new hosting. Optimisation may be more appropriate when issues result from:

  • Oversized images
  • Unnecessary plugins
  • Page builders
  • Excessive JavaScript
  • Render-blocking CSS
  • Poor caching
  • Slow external APIs

Improving the website itself may provide greater performance improvements than immediately purchasing a larger hosting plan.

Questions to Ask Before Upgrading

Performance
Which resource limits are being reached? Is caching enabled? What does PageSpeed Insights report? Is the database performing well?
Business
Does downtime affect revenue? How much data could be lost? Are backups sufficient? Is staging essential?
Hosting
Is there an intermediate plan? Can resources be upgraded without migration? Is migration assistance included? Will email be affected?
Cost
What is the renewal price? Does the cost solve a measurable problem? Will it reduce maintenance costs? Is another provider a better fit?
Decision Check

Upgrade Checklist

Consider upgrading when several of the following apply:

  • Performance remains inconsistent after optimisation
  • Resource limits are regularly exceeded
  • Downtime affects revenue
  • The website has become business-critical
  • Better backups are needed
  • Staging is required
  • Specialist WordPress support would save time
  • Growth is limited by the current plan

If only one applies occasionally, monitoring may be more appropriate than upgrading immediately.

Common Upgrade Mistakes

Upgrading because of traffic alone
Traffic should always be considered alongside workload.
Ignoring optimisation
Better hosting cannot compensate for poorly optimised websites.
Choosing the largest available plan
Purchase enough capacity for current needs with reasonable room for growth.
Forgetting renewal pricing
Always compare long-term costs rather than introductory discounts.
Assuming “managed” means “maintenance-free”
Managed hosting still requires WordPress updates, plugin management, security practices and website testing. Review the provider's documentation carefully.
Conclusion

Final Recommendation

Most websites do not need to leave shared hosting simply because they have become slightly more popular.

A shared hosting plan remains appropriate while it continues to provide reliable performance, acceptable loading times, sufficient resources, dependable backups, appropriate security and suitable support.

An upgrade becomes more worthwhile when technical limitations begin affecting visitors, business operations or development workflows.

For many growing WordPress websites, managed WordPress hosting offers a natural next step because it combines additional resources with platform features such as staging, automatic backups and WordPress-specific support.[7]

The best time to upgrade is not when marketing materials suggest you should—it is when objective evidence shows that your current hosting is limiting the website's performance, reliability or future growth.
